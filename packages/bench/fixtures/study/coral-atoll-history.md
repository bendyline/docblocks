# Ilavenu Atoll: A History, 1872–2022

Ilavenu is a ring of 23 low coral islets enclosing a lagoon 14 kilometres long. Its total land area is just 6.2 square kilometres, and its highest point, a storm ridge on the islet of Sanu, stands 3.8 metres above sea level. About 2,140 people live there today, almost all of them on Kerra, the largest islet, and its neighbour Little Kerra. This history covers the 150 years between the arrival of the first trading ship and the anniversary celebrations of 2022.

## Traders and the copra years (1872–1920)

Before 1872, roughly 400 people lived on four of the islets. They fished the lagoon and grew swamp taro in pits dug down to the freshwater lens, the layer of rainwater that floats on denser seawater inside the coral rock. In June 1872 the schooner _Merriwind_, owned by the Dellacourt Copra Company, entered the lagoon through Sanu Pass, the only channel deep enough for a ship. Its captain, Josiah Ferrant, signed an agreement with the paramount chief, Aruteva, leasing about 40 percent of Kerra's land for coconut plantations for 30 years, in return for a yearly payment in cloth, tools and iron cooking pots. The company built a store, a timber jetty and a settlement it named Port Annick, after Ferrant's daughter.

Coconut groves spread quickly, and many taro pits were filled in to make room for them. Copra, the dried coconut meat that is pressed for oil, became the atoll's only export, reaching 310 tonnes in 1890. Contact also brought disease: a measles outbreak carried by a supply ship in 1883 killed 96 people, about a quarter of the population at the time. In 1898 the teacher Miriam Oakes opened the first school, and in 1906 she and two Ilavenu assistants, Teuila Maro and Iakopo Sefa, published the first written dictionary of the Ilavenu language, with about 3,200 entries.

## Storms and the lagoon fishery (1920–1960)

Copra prices fell by more than half between 1920 and 1922. Unable to make a profit, the Dellacourt Company closed its Port Annick station in 1924 and sold the plantations back to the Ilavenu Island Council for a nominal 500 crowns. The return of the land did little for food security, because by then the islanders depended on imported rice and flour that arrived only every few months.

The worst disaster of the century came in February 1931, when a cyclone remembered locally as the Great Wind pushed a storm surge of 2.1 metres across the atoll. The surge washed completely over Little Kerra, destroyed about 80 percent of houses and poisoned the taro pits with salt. Recovery took seven years. Families rebuilt with raised floors on coral-block foundations, a design that is still common today.

In 1938 a fisherman named Netani Vau founded the Ilavenu Fishers' Union, a cooperative that salted and dried lagoon fish for sale on Calloway, the large high island 400 kilometres to the south. Within ten years the cooperative had 140 members and had replaced copra as the atoll's main source of cash. In 1952 the Larrish Protectorate, which administered the atoll, installed a radio station and weather post on Sanu, and in 1957 a rainwater programme gave every household a 2,000-litre storage tank.

## Self-government and the airstrip (1960–1995)

In 1966 the Island Council gained self-government over local affairs, and Lusia Aruteva, a great-granddaughter of the chief who had signed with Ferrant, became its first elected chair. The population peaked at about 2,600 in 1971, and crowding on Kerra became a pressing problem.

In 1974 a 1,100-metre airstrip was built on reclaimed land at Little Kerra, bringing a weekly flight to Calloway. Tourism followed, reaching 1,900 visitors in 1989. In 1978 a solid causeway was built to link Kerra and Little Kerra, but it blocked a channel that had flushed the inner lagoon. Water quality declined, and a fish kill in 1981 left thousands of dead fish along the shore. The causeway was refitted with three culverts in 1984, after which the lagoon gradually recovered. In a 1987 referendum, 78 percent of voters chose to leave the Larrish Protectorate and enter free association with Calloway, which handles defence while Ilavenu runs its own affairs.

## Rising seas and the present day (1995–2022)

A tide gauge installed on the Port Annick jetty in 1993 recorded sea level rising by an average of 4.1 millimetres a year through 2020. King tides in 2004 and 2015 flooded the remaining taro pits, and the freshwater lens has thinned; by 2010, only 9 of the atoll's 31 taro pits were still productive.

The community has responded in several ways. A 640-kilowatt solar microgrid, completed in 2012, cut diesel imports by 70 percent. A 1.8-kilometre seawall was finished along the lagoon side of Kerra in 2016. In 2019 the council bought 120 hectares of land on Calloway as "insurance land" in case relocation ever becomes necessary, although a 2021 survey found that 83 percent of residents wanted to stay on Ilavenu for as long as possible.

In June 2022, 150 years after the _Merriwind_ arrived, the atoll held a week of celebrations. The 2022 census counted 2,140 residents, and a new edition of the Oakes dictionary, expanded to 7,500 entries, was published to mark the anniversary.
