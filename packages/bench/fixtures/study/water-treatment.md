# How the Callowbrook Water Treatment Plant Works

The Callowbrook Water Treatment Plant, on the east bank of the Mirren River two kilometres north of town, supplies drinking water to about 38,000 people in Callowbrook and the neighbouring village of Ashby Ford. Opened in 1974 and rebuilt in 2011, the plant treats an average of 14.5 million litres a day, rising to 21 million litres on the hottest summer days. Water passes through five stages before it reaches a tap, and the whole journey takes about nine hours. The plant has a staff of 23, and at least two licensed operators are on site at all times.

## Intake and screening

Raw water is drawn from the Mirren through an intake tower set 4 metres below the river's average surface. That depth avoids floating debris and the warmest surface water, while staying 1.5 metres above the riverbed keeps silt out. The water first passes coarse bar screens with 50-millimetre gaps, which stop branches, leaves and plastic waste. A mechanical rake clears the bars every 20 minutes, or more often when sensors detect a drop in flow. Next come travelling band screens with 6-millimetre mesh, which remove smaller material such as reeds and small fish; a low-pressure spray returns the fish to the river through a bypass channel.

Three low-lift pumps, each rated at 300 litres per second, then lift the screened water 12 metres up to the treatment buildings, with at least one pump always kept on standby. During spring snowmelt, river turbidity can climb above 200 NTU (nephelometric turbidity units). When that happens, operators throttle back the intake and draw for up to 36 hours from the Holloway Reservoir, a 90-million-litre raw-water store that is filled during calm periods.

## Coagulation, flocculation and settling

Fine clay particles and organic matter in river water carry a negative electrical charge, so they repel one another and stay suspended. In the rapid-mix chamber, operators add aluminium sulfate (alum) at a typical dose of 25 milligrams per litre. The positively charged aluminium neutralises the particles' charge within about 30 seconds of intense mixing; this step is called coagulation.

The water then flows slowly through three flocculation basins, where paddles turning at 2 to 4 revolutions per minute encourage the destabilised particles to collide and stick together into visible clumps called floc. Flocculation takes about 30 minutes. Because alum works best in a narrow pH range, lime is added whenever the river's pH falls below 6.5, which happens most often after heavy autumn rains wash acidic peat runoff down from the Dunlow Moors.

From flocculation, the water enters two sedimentation basins, each 60 metres long and 4 metres deep. Moving at only a few centimetres per minute, it stays there for about four hours, long enough for roughly 90 percent of the floc to sink. Chain-driven scrapers push the settled sludge into hoppers. The sludge is then thickened, dewatered and trucked to a composting site at Brenner's Hill, about 1,800 tonnes of it a year.

## Filtration

Settled water still contains fine floc, some algae and microorganisms, so it next passes through eight gravity filters. Each filter bed has 60 centimetres of anthracite coal on top of 30 centimetres of fine sand, supported by graded gravel. The coarser, lighter anthracite traps larger particles near the top, while the sand catches finer ones deeper down, so the whole depth of the bed does useful work rather than just its surface. Water moves down through the media at about 8 metres per hour, and the target for filtered water is a turbidity below 0.1 NTU, well under the regulatory limit of 0.3 NTU.

As particles build up, so does resistance to flow. When the head loss across a filter reaches 2.4 metres, or after 72 hours of running, whichever comes first, the filter is backwashed: clean water is pumped upward through the bed for about 10 minutes, together with an air scour, lifting and scrubbing the media. The dirty washwater is recycled to the head of the plant rather than discharged. Turbidity spikes briefly after a backwash, so the first 15 minutes of filtered water is sent to waste. Each filter uses about 2 percent of its own output for backwashing.

## Disinfection, storage and monitoring

Filtration removes most pathogens but not all, so disinfection follows in two steps. First, the water passes through ultraviolet reactors, installed in 2019, which deliver a dose of 40 millijoules per square centimetre and inactivate chlorine-resistant parasites such as Cryptosporidium. Second, sodium hypochlorite is added to reach a free chlorine level of about 1.2 milligrams per litre. Chlorine needs time to work, so the water flows through a baffled contact tank sized to give at least 30 minutes of contact even at peak demand. The chlorine is then trimmed to 0.6 milligrams per litre for distribution: enough to protect the water in the pipes, but low enough to keep taste complaints down. Sodium hydroxide raises the pH to 7.8 to reduce corrosion of older lead and copper service lines, and fluoride is added at 0.7 milligrams per litre.

Treated water is stored in a 6-million-litre clearwell beneath the plant and pumped to two hilltop reservoirs, Tarrant Hill and Sorrel Rise, which together hold about a day's supply and feed 210 kilometres of mains by gravity.

Online analysers check turbidity, chlorine, pH and flow every 60 seconds at each stage. If filtered turbidity exceeds 0.3 NTU for more than 15 minutes, or chlorine leaving the plant drops below 0.4 milligrams per litre, an alarm sounds and the affected filter or pump shuts down automatically. Laboratory staff also take hand samples every four hours at the plant and 48 times a month from taps across the network, including schools and the hospital, and test them for coliform and E. coli bacteria. In 2023 all 576 network samples were negative for E. coli. The plant's last boil-water advisory was issued in March 2008, after a sedimentation basin failure let turbid water reach the filters.
