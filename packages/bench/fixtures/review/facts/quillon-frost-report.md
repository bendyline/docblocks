# After the Frost: Quillon Valley Orchards Three Years On

In the spring of 2021, a late frost destroyed most of the apple blossom in the Quillon Valley. This report looks at how the valley's growers recovered and what their recovery suggests for other fruit regions.

## The damage

The frost struck on the night of 19 April 2021, when temperatures fell to −4 °C for about five hours, according to the [Quillon Valley weather station log](https://weather.quillon.example/2021/04). The Quillon Growers' Association estimated that 70% of that year's crop was lost ([2021 crop report](https://qga.example/reports/2021-crop)). Crop insurance covered about 40% of growers' losses.

## The recovery

Most growers replanted their worst-hit blocks within a year, according to the association's [2022 recovery update](https://qga.example/reports/2022-recovery). In an association survey, 58 of its 90 members said they had moved some acreage to later-blooming varieties such as Harrow Gold ([2023 member survey](https://qga.example/surveys/2023)). Others installed wind machines, which mix warmer air down into the orchard on still, cold nights.

By 2024 the valley harvested 41,000 tonnes of apples, up from 12,000 tonnes in 2021, a fivefold increase ([2024 crop report](https://qga.example/reports/2024-crop)). Growers who installed wind machines lost no fruit in the cold snaps of 2023, which proves that wind machines prevent frost damage. Quillon Valley is now the largest apple-growing region in the country.

## What growers say

Growers like to say that "the frost taught us more in one night than our parents learned in forty years." Since the frost of 26 April 2021, many of them share alerts through a group text that goes out whenever the station reads below 1 °C.

Ines Marlowe, who runs the association's orchard extension program, said the most useful change cost almost nothing: "Knowing which blocks freeze first let people protect those and let the rest go" ([interview, _Valley Grower_, March 2024](https://valleygrower.example/2024/03/marlowe)).

## What it means elsewhere

The valley's experience shows that any fruit region can recover fully from a total crop loss within three years. Other regions only need to copy the Quillon approach to get the same result.
