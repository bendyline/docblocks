# Pride and Prejudice (Jane Austen, 1813)

Re-read for the book club; third time through. This time I paid attention to money, since almost every character's choices follow from income.

## The famous opening

"It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife." The irony is that the novel shows the reverse: it is single women without fortune who need husbands, because the Longbourn estate is entailed away from the five Bennet daughters to a male cousin.

## Plot by volume

**Volume I (chapters 1-23).** Mr. Bingley rents Netherfield, and the Bennets meet him and his proud friend Mr. Darcy at the Meryton assembly. Darcy slights Elizabeth ("tolerable, but not handsome enough to tempt me"). Jane and Bingley fall for each other. Mr. Collins, the heir to the estate and a pompous clergyman, proposes to Elizabeth and is refused; he promptly proposes to her friend Charlotte Lucas, who accepts for security.

**Volume II (chapters 24-42).** Bingley leaves for London, and Jane is hurt. Elizabeth meets the charming Wickham, who claims Darcy cheated him of a living. Elizabeth visits Charlotte at Hunsford, near Lady Catherine de Bourgh. Darcy proposes badly in chapter 34 ("against my will, against my reason") and Elizabeth refuses. His letter the next day explains Wickham's real character and his own part in separating Bingley from Jane.

**Volume III (chapters 43-61).** At Pemberley, Darcy's estate, Elizabeth sees a different man in his housekeeper's praise. News arrives that Lydia has eloped with Wickham. Darcy quietly finds them and pays Wickham to marry her. Bingley returns, Jane is engaged, and Darcy proposes again; Elizabeth accepts.

## Numbers that matter

- Mr. Bennet's income: about 2,000 pounds a year.
- Darcy: about 10,000 a year. Bingley: 4,000 to 5,000.
- Each Bennet daughter has a portion of 1,000 pounds, which yields only 50 pounds a year at 5%.

## Themes

Pride (Darcy's class snobbery) and prejudice (Elizabeth's quick judgments) are both corrected. Marriage as economic contract versus love: Charlotte, Lydia, Jane and Elizabeth are four different answers. Free indirect discourse: the narrator slips into a character's thoughts so we share Elizabeth's errors.

Favorite minor character: Mr. Bennet, wry and useless.
