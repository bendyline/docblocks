# Meditations by Marcus Aurelius

Read slowly over about two months, one or two entries at the start of each morning. It is really a private journal, never meant to be published, written in Greek during military campaigns on the Danube around 170-180 AD. That context changes how it reads: this is an emperor reminding himself not to be irritable.

## Structure

Twelve books. Book 1 is gratitude: he thanks the people who shaped him (his grandfather for good character, his adoptive father Antoninus Pius for steady calm, teachers, the gods). The remaining books are loose, repetitive, and often fragmentary. There is no argument that builds; the repetition is the point.

## Ideas that stuck

**The obstacle is the path.** The key line I copied onto an index card: "The impediment to action advances action. What stands in the way becomes the way." (Book 5.) Whatever blocks you gives you something to practice on.

**Dichotomy of control.** Borrowed from Epictetus. Judgment, desire and action are up to me; reputation, health, other people's behavior are not. Most distress is a mistaken judgment about the second kind.

**View from above.** Imagine looking at the earth from far up, at the crowds, the armies, the farms. Petty grievances shrink. He also reminds himself that the famous die and are forgotten too; fame lasts only a little longer than a candle.

**Memento mori.** "You could leave life right now. Let that determine what you do and say and think." Not morbid, but clarifying.

**Cooperation.** We are born for each other, like hands, feet, and eyelids. Anger at someone is acting against nature.

## Quotes I liked (short)

- "You have power over your mind, not outside events. Realize this, and you will find strength."  (paraphrased translation, widely attributed)
- "Waste no more time arguing what a good man should be. Be one." (Book 10)

## Which translation

I read the Gregory Hays version (clear, modern). The Maxwell Staniforth Penguin translation is older and more formal. Cheap free versions of the George Long translation are fine but stuffy.

## Reflection

The pattern is repeated self-correction rather than wisdom handed down. I appreciated that he never claims to have mastered it. Next in the Stoic queue: Seneca's letters, then Epictetus' Enchiridion (short).
