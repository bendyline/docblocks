# On the Origin of Species (Darwin, 1859): chapter notes

Reading the sixth edition text, which is the one most commonly available free. It is dense Victorian prose, but surprisingly readable once you realize the book is basically one long argument built from hundreds of small observations. Notes are in my own words, with a few short quotes marked.

## Background before chapter 1

Darwin sailed on HMS Beagle from December 1831 to October 1836. He returned with notebooks full of specimens and puzzles: the Galapagos mockingbirds that differed island to island, fossil armadillo-like animals in South America resembling living ones, and the sense that species were not fixed. He opened his first transmutation notebook in 1837, read Malthus in 1838, and worked out the core of natural selection that year. Then he waited. He wrote a 35-page sketch in 1842 and a 230-page essay in 1844, but did not publish. In June 1858 he received a manuscript from Alfred Russel Wallace, working in the Malay Archipelago, that described the same mechanism. A joint paper was read to the Linnean Society in July 1858, and the Origin appeared on 24 November 1859. The first printing of 1,250 copies sold out on the first day.

## Chapter 1: Variation under domestication

Darwin begins with pigeons, dogs and cabbages rather than wild nature, because everyone already accepts that breeders produce huge change. All the fancy pigeon breeds (pouters, fantails, tumblers) descend from the wild rock pigeon. Breeders select small differences and accumulate them across generations; this is artificial selection. The argument: if people can do this in a few centuries, nature could do more over geological time.

## Chapter 2: Variation under nature

Where does one draw the line between a variety and a species? Darwin says there is no sharp line; varieties are incipient species. Common, widespread, diverse genera tend to produce the most varieties.

## Chapter 3: Struggle for existence

The famous Malthusian point: more organisms are born than can survive. Even slow breeders like elephants would fill the world: one pair breeding from age 30 to 90 would, he calculated, leave around 19 million descendants alive after about 750 years. The struggle is not only predator and prey; plants at the desert's edge struggle against drought, and the struggle between individuals of the same species is the most severe, since they need the same resources.

## Chapter 4: Natural selection

The core chapter. Short statement: if variations useful to an organism occur, and if they are inherited, individuals bearing them will have a better chance of surviving and reproducing, and the favorable variations will accumulate. He coined sexual selection here, too (peacock tails). The "principle of divergence": groups diversify because different forms exploit different niches, which is why the tree of life branches. There is only one illustration in the whole book, a branching diagram in this chapter.

## Chapter 5: Laws of variation

Admits ignorance of the causes of variation and of how inheritance works. He knew nothing of genes; Mendel published in 1866 and went unnoticed until about 1900. Mentions correlated variation (blue-eyed white cats tend to be deaf) and rudimentary organs.

## Chapter 6: Difficulties on the theory

Darwin lists the objections himself: where are the transitional forms, and how could an organ as complex as the eye arise? His answer for the eye is a graded series from a simple light-sensitive patch to a complex lens eye, each step useful, and he points out that living creatures show such steps. In his words, it seems "absurd in the highest possible degree" to suppose the eye formed by selection, but reason says it could.

## Chapters 7 to 9: Instinct, hybridism, geological record

Instinct: the hive bee building hexagonal cells is a gradual evolution of simpler behavior seen in bumblebees. Hybrid sterility is not a universal marker of species. The geological record is imperfect, like a book with only a few surviving pages, which explains missing intermediates.

## A buried personal detail I liked

Darwin spent eight years on barnacles between 1846 and 1854, producing four monographs on living and fossil species. He later said it taught him what a species really is, because he saw that variation within a single barnacle species was huge. It also shows how long he let the big idea sit; some friends joked that he was hiding in the barnacles. His daughter Annie died in 1851, and some scholars think that loss, and his own illness, slowed the writing.

## Chapters 10 to 13: Distribution and classification

Chapters 11 and 12 cover biogeography. Oceanic islands lack native land mammals and amphibians but have birds, bats, insects, and so on; Darwin tested whether seeds could survive seawater (some germinated after 28 days of soaking, others after longer) and kept floating dead birds. Species on islands resemble those of the nearest mainland, not environmentally similar ones elsewhere. Chapter 13 on classification: the natural system of grouping is genealogy, with homologous structures (the bones in a bat's wing, a human arm, a whale's flipper) pointing to common descent, and embryos of different animals look alike early on.

## Chapter 14: Recapitulation and conclusion

The last paragraph has the famous line about "an entangled bank", with birds singing in the bushes, insects flitting about, worms crawling through damp earth, and how these elaborately constructed forms were all produced by laws acting around us. He ends: "There is grandeur in this view of life." The word "evolved" appears only as the last word of the book. Interesting: the first edition did not use "evolution" as a noun, and "survival of the fittest" was added later from Herbert Spencer.

## What surprised me

1. How polite and cautious Darwin is. He devotes a whole chapter to problems with his own theory.
2. How little is said about humans: a single sentence promising that "light will be thrown on the origin of man." The Descent of Man came in 1871.
3. The reliance on breeders, gardeners and pigeon fanciers as sources. He kept pigeons himself and joined London pigeon clubs.

## Reading follow-ups

Janet Browne's two-volume biography; the Beagle voyage diary (more fun, as travel writing); Jonathan Weiner's "The Beak of the Finch" for modern fieldwork on Galapagos finches.
