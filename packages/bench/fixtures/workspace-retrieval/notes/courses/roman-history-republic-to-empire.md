# Roman history: from Republic to Empire

Lecture series notes (lectures 6 through 11 merged into one file because they all feed into the same essay question). The question for the paper: "Was the fall of the Republic inevitable?" My answer so far: no, but the pressures were structural.

## The Republic's machinery

After the traditional expulsion of the last king in 509 BC, Rome was governed by annually elected magistrates. Two consuls held the top office with the power to veto each other. Below them were praetors (law courts), aediles (public works and games), quaestors (finance), and censors (the census and the Senate roll, elected roughly every five years). The Senate, a body of about 300 former magistrates, had no formal legislative power but controlled money, foreign policy and the provinces in practice. Citizens voted in assemblies that were weighted toward the rich.

In real emergencies the Republic appointed a dictator for a maximum of six months. This was a legitimate tool, not tyranny, until Sulla and later Caesar bent it. Sulla held it without a time limit from 82 BC, and Caesar was named dictator perpetuo in early 44 BC.

## Expansion and its side effects

- Three Punic Wars against Carthage: 264-241 BC (Sicily), 218-201 BC (Hannibal crossing the Alps and the defeat at Cannae in 216 BC; Scipio won at Zama in 202 BC), and 149-146 BC when Carthage was destroyed.
- Greece and Asia Minor came under Roman control through the 2nd century. Corinth was sacked in 146 BC, the same year as Carthage.
- Huge wealth and thousands of slaves flowed into Italy. Large estates (latifundia) absorbed small farms, and displaced farmers drifted to the city.

The legions had originally been drawn from property-owning citizens who served and went home. After Gaius Marius opened recruitment to the landless around 107 BC, soldiers depended on their general for land and pay after service. That shift, in my view, is the heart of the problem: loyalty moved from the state to the commander.

## The Gracchi and the breakdown of norms

Tiberius Gracchus, tribune in 133 BC, proposed redistributing public land. He was clubbed to death by senators in a riot, the first political bloodshed inside the city in generations. His brother Gaius followed a decade later (121 BC) and died too. After this, violence became a normal tool.

## Marius, Sulla, Pompey

Marius held seven consulships, Sulla marched on Rome with his army in 88 BC (the first time), and later published proscription lists in which enemies were killed and their property confiscated. Pompey made his name against pirates (67 BC) and in the East (66-62 BC), Crassus put down the Spartacus slave revolt in 71 BC.

## Caesar

Julius Caesar, Pompey and Crassus formed the informal First Triumvirate in 60 BC. Caesar's conquest of Gaul (58-50 BC) gave him enormous wealth and a loyal army. In January 49 BC he crossed the Rubicon with one legion, reportedly saying "the die is cast," beginning civil war. He defeated Pompey at Pharsalus in 48 BC, and Pompey was murdered in Egypt.

Conspirators led by Brutus and Cassius stabbed him on the Ides of March, 44 BC, in the Theatre of Pompey. There were around sixty senators in the plot. They imagined Rome would revert to normal; instead, it triggered another thirteen years of civil war.

## Octavian and the Second Triumvirate

Octavian (Caesar's adopted heir, then 18), Mark Antony and Lepidus formed the Second Triumvirate in 43 BC, this time legally, with proscriptions that killed Cicero. They beat Brutus and Cassius at Philippi in 42 BC. Octavian then outmaneuvered Lepidus, and defeated Antony and Cleopatra at the naval battle of Actium on 2 September 31 BC. Egypt became his personal possession a year later.

## Augustus: the clever settlement

In 27 BC Octavian "handed back" his powers to the Senate and received the title Augustus along with control of the provinces where most of the army was stationed. He never took the title king or dictator; he called himself princeps, "first citizen". The key legal powers were tribunician power (the right to propose and veto laws, held for life from 23 BC) and proconsular imperium.

He rebuilt the army as a professional force with 25-year service. He demobilised about 60 legions down to 28, settling roughly 100,000 veterans in colonies, and paid them from a new military treasury (aerarium militare, 6 AD) funded by taxes on inheritance and sales. He also created the Praetorian Guard as a household force in Italy, and the vigiles as a fire brigade and night watch.

Augustus died in 14 AD at 75, after a reign of more than 40 years. Boasting that he found Rome brick and left it marble, he indeed remade the city. His Res Gestae, a self-written record of his achievements, was inscribed on bronze pillars outside his mausoleum.

## Succession problems

Because the "restored Republic" was a polite fiction, there was no formal rule for passing power on. Tiberius (14-37), Caligula (37-41), Claudius (41-54) and Nero (54-68) are the Julio-Claudian line, ending in civil war in 68-69 AD, the Year of the Four Emperors. Vespasian won, founding the Flavian dynasty, and the Colosseum opened under his son Titus in 80 AD.

## The Five Good Emperors

Nerva, Trajan, Hadrian, Antoninus Pius, and Marcus Aurelius (96-180 AD), who chose adoptive heirs on merit until Marcus let his natural son Commodus succeed. Trajan's reign (98-117) saw the empire reach its greatest extent after conquering Dacia and, briefly, Mesopotamia. Hadrian's Wall in Britain began around 122 AD.

## Notes for the essay

1. Structural causes: army loyalty, land crisis, ambitious individuals outgrowing institutions designed for one-year office-holders.
2. Contingent causes: Caesar's clemency and his refusal of a bodyguard; the assassination's lack of a plan.
3. Counterpoint: Cicero's view that norms collapsed because men stopped respecting them, a moral explanation.
4. Possible thesis: the Republic could have adapted if the Senate had accepted reform after the Gracchi.

Reading list: Tom Holland's "Rubicon" (narrative), Mary Beard's "SPQR", and Syme's "The Roman Revolution" (dense, but the classic).
