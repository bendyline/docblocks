# Astronomy 101, lecture 4: how stars live and die

Lecture notes, introductory astronomy course (the Tuesday lecture, slides had a very nice H-R diagram). Lecture 9 is the separate one on planets around other stars.

## Born from collapse

Stars form when a cold molecular cloud fragment collapses under its own gravity. The protostar heats up until its core reaches roughly 10 million K, at which point hydrogen fusion begins and the object settles onto the main sequence. A star spends about 90% of its life here, fusing hydrogen into helium via the proton-proton chain (stars up to about 1.3 solar masses) or the CNO cycle (hotter, more massive ones).

Mass is destiny. Lifetime scales roughly as M^-2.5, so:

| Mass (solar) | Main-sequence life |
| --- | --- |
| 0.1 | trillions of years |
| 1 | about 10 billion years |
| 10 | about 20 million years |
| 25 | a few million years |

The Sun is about 4.6 billion years old, so roughly halfway.

## Sun-like stars: red giant to white dwarf

When hydrogen in the core runs out, the core contracts and hydrogen burns in a shell around it. The outer layers swell and cool: a red giant. At about 100 million K helium ignites in the core, in a violent helium flash for stars under about 2 solar masses. Later, helium shell burning drives pulsations and heavy mass loss.

Once the envelope drifts off as a planetary nebula, the hot dense core that remains is a white dwarf, about the size of Earth. It no longer fuses anything and just cools for billions of years. Planetary nebulae have nothing to do with planets; the name is a historical accident from the 1700s.

The Chandrasekhar limit is about 1.4 solar masses. A white dwarf above that mass cannot be held up by electron degeneracy pressure, which is why white dwarfs never exceed it (they are supported by quantum mechanics, not heat).

## Massive stars: supernovae

Stars above about 8 solar masses keep fusing heavier elements in onion-like shells: carbon, neon, oxygen, silicon, ending with iron. Iron fusion absorbs energy rather than releasing it, so the core collapses within a second and rebounds as a core-collapse (Type II) supernova. What remains is:

- a neutron star if the core is between about 1.4 and 2-3 solar masses (pulsars are spinning neutron stars), or
- a black hole above that.

A Type Ia supernova is different: a white dwarf in a binary pulls matter from its companion until it nears the Chandrasekhar limit and detonates. Because they all go off at nearly the same mass, they work as standard candles for measuring distance, which led to the discovery of accelerating expansion in 1998.

## H-R diagram reminders

The x-axis is temperature (hot on the left, which looks backwards), y is luminosity. Main sequence runs diagonally, giants sit upper right, white dwarfs lower left.

Exam question to practice: why do massive stars live shorter lives even though they have more fuel? Answer: luminosity rises steeply with mass (roughly M^3.5), so they burn through fuel much faster.
