## Prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979)

People do not evaluate outcomes as final wealth; they evaluate gains and losses relative to a reference point. Three features of the value function:

1. **Reference dependence.** Outcomes are coded as gains or losses against a starting point, such as current salary or the price paid.
2. **Loss aversion.** Losses loom larger than equal gains. The usual estimate is a ratio of about 2 to 2.25: losing $100 hurts about as much as gaining $200-225 pleases.
3. **Diminishing sensitivity.** The curve is concave for gains and convex for losses. Going from $0 to $100 feels bigger than $1,000 to $1,100.

The consequence: risk-averse over gains, risk-seeking over losses. Offered a sure $500 or a 50% chance of $1,000, most take the sure thing. Offered a sure loss of $500 or a 50% chance of losing $1,000, most gamble.

Probability weighting: small probabilities are overweighted (lottery tickets and insurance both sell), near-certain ones underweighted. This explains the certainty effect from the Allais paradox.

## Endowment effect

Thaler's mug experiment: students given a coffee mug demanded about twice as much to sell it (median around $5.25) as other students were willing to pay to buy one (about $2.75). Ownership shifts the reference point, so giving it up counts as a loss.

## Mental accounting

Richard Thaler's idea that people sort money into labeled buckets, though money is fungible.

- A person who lost a $20 theater ticket is less likely to buy another than a person who lost $20 in cash.
- Windfalls (tax refunds, bonuses) get spent more freely than salary.
- Households keep savings at 1% while carrying credit card debt at 20%.

## Framing

Same facts, different words, different choices. The Asian disease problem: "200 of 600 people will be saved" (72% choose it) versus "400 of 600 will die" (22% choose the same program).

## Sunk cost

Sunk cost fallacy: an unrecoverable outlay in the past keeps us committed to a losing course. Finishing a bad meal because it was paid for, sitting through a bad movie, escalating a failing project. A rational agent looks only at future costs and benefits.
