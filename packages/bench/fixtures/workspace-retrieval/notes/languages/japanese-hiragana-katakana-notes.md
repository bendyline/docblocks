# Japanese writing systems: hiragana, katakana, kanji

Notes from the first month of self-study. Goal: JLPT N5 next summer.

## Three scripts, used together

- Hiragana: 46 basic characters, used for native words and grammar (particles, verb endings).
- Katakana: 46 matching characters, used for loanwords, foreign names, emphasis and onomatopoeia (コーヒー coffee, テレビ television).
- Kanji: Chinese-derived characters for meaning, usually with two kinds of readings: on'yomi (Chinese-derived) and kun'yomi (native). N5 needs about 100 kanji.

A single sentence mixes all three: 私はコーヒーを飲みます (watashi wa koohii o nomimasu, "I drink coffee"). 私 and 飲 are kanji, は and を and みます are hiragana, コーヒー is katakana.

## Hiragana rows (the gojuon)

a i u e o; ka ki ku ke ko; sa shi su se so; ta chi tsu te to; na ni nu ne no; ha hi fu he ho; ma mi mu me mo; ya yu yo; ra ri ru re ro; wa wo; n.

Dakuten (two small marks) voices a consonant: か ka becomes が ga, は ha becomes ば ba. Handakuten (a small circle) turns h into p: は becomes ぱ pa.

Small っ doubles the next consonant (きって kitte, a stamp). Small ゃ ゅ ょ combine with i-column characters: きゃ kya, しゅ shu, ちょ cho. A long vowel is written with an extra vowel in hiragana (おおきい ookii) and with ー in katakana.

## Look-alikes that tripped me up

- ぬ (nu) and め (me)
- わ (wa), れ (re) and ね (ne)
- シ (shi) and ツ (tsu); ソ (so) and ン (n)

Trick: for シ and ツ, the strokes of shi are more horizontal and tsu's are more vertical.

## Method

Wrote each kana ten times following stroke order, then used a spaced repetition deck. About 20 characters a day got me through hiragana in a week and a half.
