# 401(k) rollover checklist

When leaving a job, the old plan balance has four possible fates: leave it, roll it to the new employer's plan, roll it to an IRA, or cash out (almost always a bad idea).

## Direct vs indirect

A direct rollover sends the check payable to the new custodian, so nothing is withheld. It is a trustee-to-trustee transfer: the check is made out to something like "Fidelity FBO My Name" and no income tax is due.

An indirect rollover pays me first. The plan must withhold 20% for federal tax, and I have 60 days to deposit the full original amount into the new account (so I have to make up the 20% from other money to avoid it being treated as a distribution). Miss the window and it is taxable income plus the 10% penalty if under 59 and a half. Always choose direct.

## Steps

1. Open the receiving account first (rollover IRA, or the new plan).
2. Request the distribution form from the old plan administrator; choose "direct rollover".
3. Confirm whether the old balance is pre-tax, Roth, or both. They must go to matching destinations (pre-tax to traditional IRA, Roth 401(k) to Roth IRA), otherwise the transfer becomes a taxable conversion.
4. Watch for after-tax contributions in the plan; these can sometimes be split to a Roth.
5. Keep the paperwork. The 1099-R comes in January with code G for a direct rollover.

## IRA vs new plan: tradeoffs

- IRA: more investment choices, usually lower-cost index funds, but no loan option and weaker creditor protection in some states.
- New employer plan: keeps the money in one place, allows loans, and can enable the backdoor Roth (an IRA with pre-tax money triggers the pro-rata rule).
- Old plan: fine if fees are low; some plans offer institutional share classes at 0.03% that are cheaper than retail.

## Rule of 55

If I separate from the employer in or after the year I turn 55, I can take penalty-free withdrawals from that employer's 401(k), but not from an IRA. Rolling over would lose that option.

## Company stock

If the old 401(k) has employer stock, net unrealized appreciation (NUA) rules may allow a lower capital-gains rate; talk to a tax professional before rolling it.
