# Index funds: why and how

## What it is

An index fund holds all (or a representative sample of) the securities in a market index and tries to match its return, rather than picking winners. A total-market stock fund holds thousands of companies; an S&P 500 fund holds about 500 large US companies, weighted by market capitalization.

## Why it works

1. **Costs.** Expense ratios of 0.03% to 0.10% for broad index funds versus roughly 0.5% to 1% for active funds. On $100,000 over 30 years at 7% gross, a 1% annual fee costs about $175,000 in final balance compared with the cheap fund. Fees compound as hard as returns.
2. **Persistence data.** The SPIVA scorecards show that over 15 years roughly 85% to 90% of active large-cap US funds underperform the S&P 500.
3. **Tax efficiency.** Low turnover means few capital gain distributions in taxable accounts. ETFs add the in-kind redemption mechanism.
4. **Simplicity.** No need to guess the manager.

## ETF vs mutual fund

Both can track an index. ETFs trade throughout the day at market price and have no minimums (fractional shares at most brokers). Mutual funds trade once a day at net asset value and may have investment minimums such as $3,000. In a retirement account, the difference barely matters.

## A simple portfolio

The three-fund idea: US total stock market, international total stock market, and a total bond fund. A common age-based rule of thumb: bond percentage equals age (or age minus 20 for the more aggressive). Rebalance once a year or when an allocation drifts 5 percentage points.

## Behavior beats cleverness

Dollar-cost averaging into a fund on a fixed schedule avoids trying to time the market. Past data show missing the ten best days over twenty years can roughly halve the final result, and the best days cluster next to the worst ones.

## Caveats

Index funds do fall: the S&P 500 dropped about 34% in 33 days in early 2020 and about 57% from 2007 to 2009 before recovering. Cap-weighting means a few large stocks dominate the fund.
