# Roth vs traditional retirement accounts

Concept note, not advice for anyone else. Numbers are the 2025 limits; they change yearly with inflation, so recheck the IRS page each January.

## The core difference

The only question is when the tax bill arrives.

| | Traditional | Roth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contribution | pre-tax (deductible) | after-tax |
| Growth | tax-deferred | tax-free |
| Withdrawals in retirement | taxed as ordinary income | tax-free if qualified |
| Required minimum distributions | start at age 73 (75 if born 1960 or later) | none for Roth IRA (Roth 401(k) no longer has them either from 2024) |

Rule of thumb: if my tax rate now is higher than I expect in retirement, traditional wins; if lower, Roth wins. If unsure, split between the two for tax diversification.

## Limits (2025)

- IRA (traditional or Roth combined): $7,000, plus $1,000 catch-up at 50 or older.
- 401(k) employee deferral: $23,500, plus $7,500 catch-up at 50 or older.

## Income limits for the Roth IRA

For 2025 the direct Roth IRA contribution phases out between $150,000 and $165,000 of modified AGI for single filers. For married filing jointly it is $236,000 to $246,000. Above that, the "backdoor Roth" is the workaround: contribute to a non-deductible traditional IRA, then convert. The pro-rata rule taxes the conversion proportionally if I have other pre-tax IRA money, which is the trap.

Traditional IRA deductibility also phases out if I (or my spouse) have a workplace plan.

## Withdrawal rules

Basis can be withdrawn at any time, tax-free and penalty-free; only the growth is locked up. Earnings come out tax-free only if the account is at least five years old and I am 59 and a half or older (or disabled, or a first-time home purchase up to $10,000). Early withdrawals of earnings face income tax plus a 10% penalty, with exceptions such as certain medical costs, qualified education, and a series of substantially equal payments.

Traditional accounts: any pre-59.5 withdrawal is taxed plus 10% penalty, with similar exceptions.

## Other facts

- A Roth conversion is taxable in the year it happens; the five-year clock for each conversion is separate.
- Employer match always goes pre-tax unless the plan offers Roth match (rare, since SECURE 2.0).
- Roth IRAs can be inherited tax-free but beneficiaries generally must empty them within ten years.
